Toxicology

Toxicology studies the adverse effects of chemicals on living organisms, by investigating the relationship between dose and effect, dose-response, and route of admission.

“The doses make the poison” - Paracelsus 1500-ish

Predictive Toxicology

Predictive (computational) toxicology combines computational power with the principles of toxicology to infer the risks associated with chemicals that are not yet extensively studied or for extrapolating toxicity data from one species to another.

New Appraoch Methodologies (NAM), replaces traditional animal testing with non-mammalian models, like C. elegans, Drosophila, Daphnia, cell lines (in vitro “in glass” lat.), and solely computationally (machine learning). These methods are faster, cheaper and reduce number of animals tested! The results are translated into underlying physiological mechanisms that lead to prediction of adverse effects in humans.

High-Throughput Screening (HTS), used to test as many chemicals as fast as possible. This include improving the wet lab, data gathering, data processing, prediction and forming conclusions. Here cell-assays and automated computational pipelines are play a significant role.

Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship (QSAR)

QSAR is a computational modeling method used to predict the biological activity of chemical compounds based on their molecular structure.

Concept: similar structures == similar activities.

If we know how a set of molecules behave biologically, we can build a mathematical model that links their structural features to that behavior — then use it to predict how new, untested molecules might behave.

Numerical descriptors

Numerical values (vectors, strings, matrix) that encode structural and physicochemical properties of a molecule:

  • Molecular weight, logP (lipophilicity), polar surface area
  • Number of hydrogen bond donors/acceptors
  • Topological indices (how atoms are connected)
  • Electronic properties (charge distribution)
  • 3D shape features

These are the independent variable, the x

Mathematical model

Historically, simple linear regression was used. Modern QSAR uses random forests, support vector machines, neural networks, and more.

  • Linear regression
  • Partial least squares
  • Discriminant analysis
  • Random forests (ML)
  • Deep neural networks (ML)

Chemical structures are translated into numerical descriptors, such as molecular weight (physicochemical), molecular fingerprints (topological), and partial charges or frontier orbital energies (electronic), which are then correlated with toxicological endpoints like acute toxicity, bioactivity, enzyme inhibition or receptor binding.

Traditional QSAR approaches employ linear regression, partial least squares, or discriminant analysis, while modern QSAR increasingly relies on machine learning techniques such as random forests, support vector machines, and deep neural networks. QSAR models are widely used for screening large chemical libraries, prioritizing substances for testing, and supporting regulatory decision-making, particularly when experimental data are sparse or unavailable.

Physico-chemical properties = Physical + Solvation + Molecular Attributes

Physical properties include freezing point, boiling point, melting point, infrared spectrum, electronic parameters, viscosity, and density. Some of these physical properties (e.g., electronic parameters, molecular weight, boiling/freezing point) are directly associated with environmental fate and health effects.

Solvation properties14 describe a chemical’s interactions with different phases and its partitioning between phases. Solvation properties of interest in alternatives assessment can be divided into three main types: (a) phase partitioning, (b) solubility, and (c) colligative properties: + A partition-coefficient or distribution-coefficient is defined mathematically as the ratio of concentrations of a given compound across two mixed, immiscible phases at equilibrium. In the context of a chemical alternatives assessment, important partition coefficients are often measured in the liquid phase. Though partitioning can be measured across a range of solvents and phases, the phase partition coefficient most often encountered when assessing physicochemical properties is from a system where one solvent is water or an aqueous phase and the second is organic and hydrophobic, such as 1-octanol (i.e., octanol/water partition coefficient [Kow] represented by P).15 + Solubility. This chemical property refers to the ability of a given substance (the solute) to dissolve in a solvent. The primary measurement of interest in chemical alternatives assessment is solubility in water. + Colligative properties. Colligative properties are properties of solutions that are not dependent on the chemical species but instead on the ratio of the number of solute particles to the number of solvent molecules in a solution. Examples of colligative properties include lowering of vapor pressure, elevation of boiling point, and depression of freezing point. Colligative properties generally do not play a significant role in alternatives assessments and are not discussed further in this report. The term molecular attribute is used to describe properties related to molecular shape and size. For the purposes of this report, the committee considers electronic parameters of molecules (e.g., frontier orbital energies and polarizability) that affect chemical reactivity as a type of molecular attribute.

Machine Learning

Machine Learning (ML) is a sub-dicipline of Artificial Intelligence (AI) that combines mathematical algorithms and statistical models to perform predictions without explicit programming on datasets. The machine “learns” how to analyse a datasets by finding common patterns. There are different learning methods:

  • Supervised Learning: the algorithm learns from labeled dataset, both input and outout is provided (neutral networks)
  • Unsupervised Learning: the algorithm learns from unlabeled dataset (clustering, dimensionality reduction)

Neural Networks (NN) is a ML model which “learns” a dataset by adjusting many many parameters to “fit” the outcome of the dataset.

Clustering K-means

Clustering is a technique used to group similar objects into clusters. It is an unsupervised learning problem, meaning it makes inferences from datasets using only input vectors without referring to known, or labeled, outcomes. The aim of clustering is to partition a set of objects into K clusters in such a way that the sum of the squared distances between the objects and their assigned cluster mean is minimized. Clusters are thus internally homogeneous and distinct from each other.

Dimensionality reduction PCA

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a linear dimensionality reduction technique widely used in exploratory data analysis, visualization, and data preprocessing. The aim is to reduce dimensions based on variation within the varibles, clustering together variables that are similar to eachother.
